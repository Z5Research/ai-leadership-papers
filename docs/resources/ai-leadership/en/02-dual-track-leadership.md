# From Situational Leadership to Cognitive Management: The Paradigm Shift in AI-Era Management

---

## Opening: The Same Key, Two Different Locks

Lin Wei was a product director at an internet company. She had led a team of twelve for three years.

After AI tools were fully deployed last year, she noticed something eerie: the harder she tried her old management methods, the more problems emerged in the team.

She gave autonomy to Zhang Wei, the smartest person on her team—"You lead this project. I don't care about the process, only the results." Zhang Wei was energized, her output doubled, her PPTs became more polished, her proposals more comprehensive. But one late night three months later, Lin Wei reviewed Zhang Wei's proposal drafts and discovered that all core judgments were AI-generated. Zhang Wei herself could not clearly explain what user problem this feature actually solved.

She gave constraints to Li Zhe, the most reliable person on her team—"Don't always use AI. Think it through yourself before discussing." Li Zhe agreed, but the quality of his submissions clearly declined. He began to feel anxious, and his silence during meetings grew longer.

Sitting in her office, Lin Wei felt, for the first time, a deep sense of powerlessness.

The situational leadership she had learned before told her: match management style to employees' capability levels—give direction to those with low capability, give space to those with high capability. She had used this logic for three years, and it worked well.

But now, the same management actions produced completely opposite effects on Zhang Wei and Li Zhe. Autonomy made Zhang Wei drift further; constraints made Li Zhe sink deeper.

She was no longer facing the question of "who is strong and who is weak." She was facing two completely different cognitive patterns—and these two patterns, catalyzed by AI, were evolving at different speeds.

This was not Lin Wei's dilemma alone. It was a structural challenge that all of management must face in the AI era.

## Part I: Why Situational Leadership Fails in the AI Era

### 1.1 The Linear Assumption of the Hersey-Blanchard Model

In 1969, Paul Hersey and Ken Blanchard proposed Situational Leadership Theory. This model became one of the core contents of management education for the next fifty years. Its core logic is简洁而优雅:

An employee's "Readiness" is a continuum from low to high, divided into four stages—D1 (low capability, high willingness), D2 (low capability, low willingness), D3 (high capability, unstable willingness), D4 (high capability, high willingness). Managers match one of four management styles to the employee's stage: Telling, Selling, Participating, Delegating.

The underlying assumption of this model is: **employee capability is linear, rankable, and improvable.**

Linear means you can place every person on the team along an axis from D1 to D4. Rankable means you can clearly say "Zhang San is more capable than Li Si." Improvable means that through training and coaching, employees can progress from D1 to D4.

Under this assumption, the manager's task is diagnosis—finding each person's position on the continuum, then matching the corresponding management style.

This model became popular because it perfectly matched the organizational reality from the industrial era to the early knowledge era. During that period, capability differences among employees were mainly reflected in experience accumulation and skill proficiency—linear, accumulative variables. A ten-year experienced engineer was indeed "more capable" than a one-year experienced engineer, and this difference was continuous and predictable.

### 1.2 How AI Broke the Linearity

The proliferation of AI tools has destroyed this linear assumption.

Zhang Wei, a product manager with less than a year of tenure, can produce D4-level work output with AI assistance—logically rigorous proposals, data-rich analysis, beautifully formatted PPTs. By output, she is a D4 employee.

But by cognitive pattern, her decision sequence is "ask AI first, then decide whether to adopt"—a classic D1 characteristic: dependence on external directives, lack of independent judgment.

The Hersey-Blanchard model encounters here a paradox it cannot handle: **output and capability have decoupled.**

When AI becomes a capability lever, employee capability is no longer a single dimension. An employee might be D4 in "output quality," D1 in "cognitive independence," and D2 in "systems thinking." These three dimensions are no longer correlated—they may even be negatively correlated: the more output depends on AI, the lower cognitive independence.

Management textbooks have never discussed this situation.

Peter Drucker wrote in *The Practice of Management*: "Management is a practice, whose essence lies not in knowing but in doing." Drucker emphasized the action orientation of managers—adjusting management style based on employees' actual performance. But the problem in the AI era is that employees' "actual performance" no longer truthfully reflects their cognitive state.

The "good performance" you see may be AI's credit, not the employee's capability. The "poor performance" you see may be the growing pains of an employee trying to break AI dependence, not capability degradation.

Output—the once most reliable diagnostic indicator—has now become the most unreliable one.

### 1.3 From Uniform Distribution to Bimodal Distribution

Traditional management also has an implicit assumption: team capability structure follows an approximately uniform distribution. Most employees are at the middle level, with a few at the extremes. Managers find an "optimal solution" and promote it across the team, achieving overall improvement.

The AI era has彻底 changed this distribution pattern.

Research published nearly simultaneously by Stanford HAI and McKinsey in 2025 points to the same phenomenon: the proliferation of AI tools has not uniformly improved everyone's capabilities, but has split teams into two groups—AI-augmented and AI-dependent. The capability gap between these two groups is not gradual; it is断裂.

This is not a continuum. This is a **bimodal distribution**.

Under a bimodal distribution, the management logic of "find the optimal solution and promote it" fails. Because management actions effective for augmented types (autonomy, challenge) are poison for dependent types—they will use autonomy to further depend on AI, use challenges to verify AI output rather than think independently. Management actions effective for dependent types (constraints, guidance) are束缚 for augmented types—they will feel untrusted and then find other battlefields that don't require you.

One set of methods simultaneously harms both ends. This is the structural dilemma of managers in the AI era.

## Part II: From Capability Management to Cognitive Pattern Management

### 2.1 The Core Proposition of the Cognitive Management Paradigm

When output and capability decouple, when capability distribution shifts from uniform to bimodal, management needs a new paradigm to understand "how to manage a team reshaped by AI."

I call it the **Cognitive Management Paradigm**.

Its core proposition can be stated in one sentence: **Management in the AI era is no longer about "matching capability levels," but about "matching cognitive patterns."**

This sounds like wordplay, but its meaning runs far deeper than the surface.

"Capability level" is a result variable. It answers the question "What can this person do?" The situational leadership model is built on this variable—what you can do determines what management style I give you.

"Cognitive pattern" is a process variable. It answers the question "How does this person think?" It focuses on decision sequence (think first or ask AI first), attitude toward tools (find faults or accept everything), state after leaving tools (clear direction or模糊 direction), and capability change trends (whether higher-order capabilities are improving or basic capabilities are degrading).

These two variables are no longer correlated in the AI era. A person with a D1 cognitive pattern (dependent) can produce D4-level work. A person with a D4 cognitive pattern (independent) might produce D2-level work because they refuse to use AI.

When you still diagnose employees using "capability level," you are diagnosing an indicator already contaminated by AI. What you need is a diagnostic dimension unaffected by AI—cognitive pattern.

### 2.2 Capability Can Be Trained; Cognitive Patterns Require Structural Intervention

The optimism of traditional management lies in its assumption: all problems can be solved through training. Low capability? Train. Low willingness? Motivate. This logic holds under the premise that capability is a linear variable—give a D1 employee enough training and coaching, and they will eventually reach D4.

The Cognitive Management Paradigm proposes a less optimistic but more truthful proposition: **cognitive patterns cannot be changed through training; they require structural intervention.**

A dependent employee's problem is not "cannot think independently"—they may have been able to before using AI. Their problem is "the neural pathways for independent thinking have degenerated." This is not a knowledge problem; it is a habit problem. You cannot reverse an established habit through a "critical thinking class."

Structural intervention means changing the environment, changing the rules, changing incentive mechanisms. It is not "teaching them how to do it," but "designing an environment where they must do it themselves."

Setting "No-AI Periods" is structural intervention. Requiring "visible thinking process" is structural intervention. Introducing "Cognitive Contribution" indicators in performance evaluation is structural intervention.

These actions share a common characteristic: they do not try to change the cognitive pattern itself, but change the environment in which the cognitive pattern survives. When the environment no longer rewards dependent behavior, cognitive patterns will begin to shift.

### 2.3 From Management Style to Management Actions

Situational leadership provides "management styles"—Telling, Selling, Participating, Delegating. These are macro, directional categories.

The Cognitive Management Paradigm provides "management actions"—constrain, awaken, delegate, challenge. These are specific, actionable, and combinable behaviors.

This shift is not a semantic preference, but an inevitable result of improved diagnostic precision.

When you locate an employee on a continuum, "style" is enough—you only need to know if they are D1 or D4, then match Telling or Delegating.

When you face two cognitive patterns in a bimodal distribution, "style" is no longer enough—you need to know when to delegate and when to challenge augmented types; when to constrain and when to awaken dependent types. The combination, sequencing, and intensity of these actions determine management effectiveness.

Henry Mintzberg described in *The Nature of Managerial Work* that manager roles are fragmented and reactive—managers are not executing a unified "style," but making different action choices in different situations. The AI era amplifies this fragmentation: the same manager, at different times of the same day, may need to take "delegation" actions for augmented employees and "constraint" actions for dependent employees. These two actions are opposite in direction, but under the Cognitive Management Paradigm, both are correct.

## Part III: Three Theoretical Pillars of Dual-Track Leadership

### 3.1 Pillar I: Cognitive Heterogeneity Hypothesis (认知异质性假设)

The first theoretical pillar of Dual-Track Leadership is the **Cognitive Heterogeneity Hypothesis**.

Its statement is simple: **In AI-era teams, different cognitive patterns will inevitably coexist. Managers should not pursue cognitive uniformity, but should manage cognitive diversity.**

This assumption challenges a deep belief of traditional management. From Taylor's scientific management to Drucker's management by objectives, from Deming's quality management to Google's Project Oxygen, the mainstream tradition of management has been pursuing "unified best practices"—finding one best way to manage, then standardizing and promoting it across the organization.

This pursuit is reasonable under the uniform distribution assumption. If team capability differences are continuous, then an optimal management style does exist—it lies near the median of the distribution, effective for most people.

But under a bimodal distribution, the "optimal management style" does not exist. Because the area between the two peaks is empty—no employee is in a "middle state." Any attempt to find a "universal method" will simultaneously deviate from both peaks.

The Cognitive Heterogeneity Hypothesis requires managers to accept a counterintuitive reality: **cognitive differences in the team are not noise to be eliminated, but assets to be managed.**

Augmented employees and dependent employees are not "good employees" vs. "poor employees." They are two different cognitive niches—under appropriate management actions, each can create different types of value for the organization. Augmented types are suited for exploring the unknown, breaking boundaries, and undertaking high-risk, high-reward projects. Dependent types are suited for executing standardized processes, ensuring output stability, and operating efficiently under clear rules.

The manager's task is not to turn dependent types into augmented types—that is impossible and unnecessary. The manager's task is to find the optimal management actions for each peak in the bimodal distribution, then let both peaks coexist within the same organization.

### 3.2 Pillar II: Management Action Inversion (管理动作反向性)

The second theoretical pillar of Dual-Track Leadership is **Management Action Inversion**.

Its statement is: **For augmented and dependent employees, effective management actions are opposite in direction. Push augmented types; pull dependent types.**

"Push" and "pull" are not rhetoric. They describe two fundamentally different management dynamics.

The "push" for augmented employees centers on delegation and challenge. Delegation means pushing decision authority into their hands—not "you propose, I approve," but "you decide, I back you up." Challenge means pushing difficulty onto them—not "do more," but "do harder."

The greatest risk for augmented employees is not insufficient capability, but cognitive drift—AI creates the illusion that "I am very strong," leading them to depend on AI even in areas where they don't need it. The push action counteracts this drift: using larger battlefields to force them to face problems AI cannot solve, using harder questions to force them to deploy genuine judgment.

The "pull" for dependent employees centers on constraint and awakening. Constraint means raising the threshold for AI use—from "can use anytime" to "must first submit own plan." Awakening means pulling them out of AI's comfort zone—not through criticism, but through designing "small victories" to rebuild confidence in independent thinking.

The greatest risk for dependent employees is not output decline, but cognitive atrophy—they are not incapable, they just haven't used their capabilities for too long. The pull action counteracts this atrophy: using structural constraints to create scenarios where they "must think for themselves," using positive feedback to rebuild the belief that "I can also do well."

Push and pull are opposite in direction but aligned in goal: **keep both cognitive patterns in a healthy state, preventing cognitive drift and cognitive atrophy.**

This inversion principle explains why situational leadership fails in the AI era—because it assumes management action direction is unidirectional: from D1 to D4, management style moves from Telling to Delegating. But under a bimodal distribution, both directions are needed: push augmented types further toward "Delegating," pull dependent types toward "Telling." Two endpoints on the same continuum require opposite actions.

### 3.3 Pillar III: The Cognitive Translator Role (认知翻译者)

The third theoretical pillar of Dual-Track Leadership is the **Cognitive Translator**.

Its statement is: **In cognitively heterogeneous teams, the manager's core role is not referee (judging who is right or wrong), but translator (building understanding between two cognitive patterns).**

This role is based on a phenomenon overlooked by management literature: **conflicts between cognitive patterns are not interest conflicts, but understanding conflicts.**

Augmented employees see dependent employees as "unambitious"—having tools available but refusing to use them, insisting on slow independent thinking. Dependent employees see augmented employees as "opportunistic"—having their own work to do but delegating it to AI.

Both types use their own cognitive pattern as the standard of judgment. This is not a moral issue; it is a cognitive blind spot.

The manager's translator role manifests at three levels:

**Level 1: Language Translation.** Augmented employees are accustomed to language like "efficiency," "leverage," and "iteration." Dependent employees are accustomed to language like "steadiness," "caution," and "following process." Managers need to build mappings between these two languages—helping augmented employees understand that "steadiness" is not "inefficiency," and helping dependent employees understand that "leverage" is not "laziness."

**Level 2: Value Translation.** Augmented employees' value lies in breakthrough and innovation. Dependent employees' value lies in stability and predictability. Managers need to accommodate both values in the organization's evaluation system—neither letting "innovation" become the sole standard nor "stability" the sole pursuit.

**Level 3: Cognitive Translation.** This is the deepest level. Managers need to let both cognitive patterns see each other's thinking processes. Augmented employees need to see the caution and thoroughness behind dependent employees' "slowness." Dependent employees need to see the judgment and trade-offs behind augmented employees' "speed." When cognitive patterns shift from "mutual judgment" to "mutual understanding," the team truly moves from division to integration.

Mintzberg proposed that managers' "interpersonal roles" include figurehead, leader, and liaison. The Cognitive Translator is an extension of Mintzberg's framework in the AI era—when teams are divided by cognitive patterns, the liaison role is no longer simple information transfer, but the translation and reconstruction of cognitive frameworks.

## Part IV: Practice — Implementing Cognitive Management in Organizations

### 4.1 Diagnostic Tool: Four-Dimensional AI Usage Behavior Diagnostic Table

The value of theory lies in diagnosis. The first step of the Cognitive Management Paradigm is diagnosis—not diagnosing employees' capability levels, but diagnosing their cognitive patterns.

Below is a four-dimensional diagnostic framework. Each dimension has two extremes; managers locate employees on each dimension by observing their behavior (not asking about their attitudes).

**Dimension I: Decision Sequence**

| Augmented Direction | Behavioral Feature | Dependent Direction |
|---|---|---|
| Forms own judgment first, then uses AI to verify or challenge | Has core viewpoints ready before meetings, uses AI to find loopholes | Asks AI first, then decides whether to adopt |

Diagnostic method: In a 1-on-1 meeting, ask one question—"Before you got AI's help on this proposal, what did you think of yourself?" Observe whether the person needs to think to answer or can immediately state their original idea.

**Dimension II: Attitude Toward AI Output**

| Augmented Direction | Behavioral Feature | Dependent Direction |
|---|---|---|
| Finds faults, identifies loopholes, synthesizes multiple plans | Says to AI output "This is wrong, because..." | Accepts, tweaks, directly adopts |

Diagnostic method: Observe employees' first reaction after receiving AI output. Questioning or accepting? Modifying or copying?

**Dimension III: State After Leaving AI**

| Augmented Direction | Behavioral Feature | Dependent Direction |
|---|---|---|
| Efficiency drops but direction remains clear | Works slower without AI, but knows what they are doing | Efficiency drops and direction becomes模糊 |

Diagnostic method: Design a "No-AI Work Period" in the team (e.g., a two-hour brainstorming meeting, banning all AI tools), and observe different employees' performance.

**Dimension IV: Capability Change Trend After AI Usage**

| Augmented Direction | Behavioral Feature | Dependent Direction |
|---|---|---|
| Higher-order capabilities (judgment, systems thinking) improving | Can identify AI's logical errors | Basic capabilities (analytical ability, expression) degrading |

Diagnostic method: Track employees' capability changes over three months. Not by output quantity, but by output "cognitive density"—how many judgments were made by the employee themselves?

After diagnosis, synthesize each employee's performance across four dimensions to get a rough positioning: "leaning augmented" or "leaning dependent." Precise classification is not needed—the purpose of diagnosis is to guide management actions, not to label people.

**Important reminder: Cognitive patterns are context-dependent.** The same employee might be augmented when writing code but dependent when making presentations. Diagnosis must be specific to work scenarios, not at the personality level.

### 4.2 Management Action Library: Dual-Track Action Combinations

After diagnosis, managers need to select management actions for different employee types. Below is a structured action library.

**Action Library for Augmented Employees (Push Direction):**

| Action Category | Specific Action | Applicable Scenario | Risk Warning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Delegate | "You decide, I back you up"—fully hand over decision authority | Employee has demonstrated independent judgment ability | Prevent delegation from becoming neglect—still need regular reviews |
| Delegate | Let employee lead projects rather than participate | Project complexity is moderate, employee has team leadership experience | Avoid giving critical projects to employees experiencing cognitive drift |
| Challenge | Assign tasks beyond comfort zone | Employee feels bored with current tasks | Challenge difficulty must be controllable—too easy is meaningless, too hard provides no feedback |
| Challenge | Question employee where they are most confident | Employee is overconfident about a proposal | Purpose of questioning is to stimulate thinking, not to打击 confidence |
| Challenge | Let augmented employees teach dependent employees | Clear cognitive differences exist in the team | Teaching process must be structured—not simply "mentoring" |

**Action Library for Dependent Employees (Pull Direction):**

| Action Category | Specific Action | Applicable Scenario | Risk Warning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Constrain | Set "No-AI Periods"—ban AI tools during specific time periods | Employee shows clear AI dependence | No-AI periods should not be too long—start from 30 minutes, gradually extend |
| Constrain | Require "visible thinking process"—submit own ideas before using AI | Employee output quality is high but cognitive independence is low | Avoid becoming formalistic—focus is on thinking process, not document format |
| Constrain | Gradually raise AI usage threshold | Employee's AI dependence is affecting basic capabilities | Raising speed must be controllable—too aggressive triggers resistance |
| Awaken | Design "small victories"—let employee experience success without AI | Employee has lost confidence in independent thinking | Small victory difficulty must be precisely matched—too simple provides no sense of achievement, too hard provides no positive feedback |
| Awaken | Establish "thinking partner" relationship rather than superior-subordinate | Employee resists constraints | Core of thinking partner is empathy, not sympathy—convey "I believe you can do it" not "I pity your inability" |

**Principles of Action Combination:**

Augmented employees' action combination is "delegate primarily, challenge secondarily." First delegate, observe whether cognitive drift appears. If so, add challenge actions. The purpose of challenge is to convert the freedom from delegation into growth, not to withdraw delegation.

Dependent employees' action combination is "constrain primarily, awaken secondarily." First constrain, creating an environment where they "must think for themselves." Simultaneously use awakening actions to provide positive feedback, preventing constraints from becoming punishment.

### 4.3 Integration Mechanisms: Keeping Dual Tracks from Dividing

The greatest risk of Dual-Track Leadership is team division. When managers take different actions toward different people, employees will perceive "unfairness"—why can Zhang Wei make autonomous decisions, but Li Zhe needs approval even to use AI?

Managers need to design integration mechanisms to resolve this perception.

**Mechanism I: Mixed Grouping**

Place augmented and dependent employees in the same project team. Not to let the former lead the latter—but to let them mutually calibrate through collaboration.

Augmented employees will learn patience and detail control from dependent employees. Dependent employees will learn judgment and systematic thinking from augmented employees.

The key to mixed grouping is role design—augmented employees should not naturally become "team leads," and dependent employees should not naturally become "executors." Each person's unique contribution must be clearly defined at the project's start, ensuring both cognitive patterns have irreplaceable value in the project.

**Mechanism II: AI Usage Transparency**

Build a culture within the team: everyone openly shares how they use AI. Not to show off "how much I used," but to honestly share "where I used it, why I used it, and what the results were."

When AI usage shifts from black box to transparency, dependent employees will see augmented employees' thinking processes—they will discover that augmented employees don't "not use AI," but "don't use AI for key judgments." Augmented employees will also become aware of their own blind spots—they will discover that dependent employees are more precise in certain detail controls.

**Mechanism III: Regular "De-AI Days"**

Choose one day each month when all team members disable AI tools. Not punishment, but calibration.

For augmented employees, De-AI Days are a "baseline capability checkup"—confirming whether their judgment, analytical ability, and expression skills still exist. For dependent employees, De-AI Days are an "independent walking experience"—discovering they can actually do many things, they just haven't done them for too long.

For managers, De-AI Days are a "diagnostic opportunity"—without AI interference, you can more accurately see each employee's true cognitive pattern.

### 4.4 Restructuring Performance Management

The most profound management challenge in the AI era occurs in performance evaluation.

Gartner's 2025 survey of 300 enterprises shows that 68% of managers admit "AI tool usage has made performance evaluation more difficult." The core pain point: you cannot distinguish "AI's credit" from "human credit."

A dependent employee uses AI to write ten copy pieces per day. An augmented employee uses AI to assist deep thinking and writes only three. By output quantity, the former overwhelms the latter. By business value, the latter's three pieces may bring genuine strategic breakthroughs, while the former's ten pieces merely fill the content pool.

**Your performance system is rewarding the wrong people.**

The Cognitive Management Paradigm requires performance management to shift from "output-oriented" to "cognitive process-oriented." Specifically, a new evaluation dimension must be introduced—the **Cognitive Contribution Index (CCI)**.

Cognitive Contribution Index consists of three sub-indicators:

**Sub-indicator I: Independent Judgment Density.** Of the employee's output, how many key judgments were made independently? This indicator can be measured through the "visible thinking process" mechanism—requiring employees to submit a "decision log" alongside their output, recording where judgments were made and the basis for them.

**Sub-indicator II: AI Usage Quality.** Not "how much AI was used," but "how AI was used." Augmented employees have high AI usage quality—they let go in AI's strong areas and persist in their own. Dependent employees have low AI usage quality—they delegate even judgment areas to AI. This indicator can be assessed through AI usage transparency.

**Sub-indicator III: Cognitive Growth Rate.** Has the employee's cognitive pattern undergone positive change over a period (e.g., a quarter)? Augmented employees' cognitive growth is reflected in improved judgment. Dependent employees' cognitive growth is reflected in restored independence. This indicator requires longitudinal tracking.

Cognitive Contribution Index is not meant to replace traditional output indicators—it is meant to supplement their blind spots. In the AI era, a complete performance evaluation should answer two questions: "What did this person produce?" and "How did this person produce it?"

An HBR article published in 2025, "Managing Beyond Performance Metrics in the AI Era," points out: when AI makes output cheap, managers' attention should shift from "output itself" to "the cognitive process of output." This is not a denial of performance management, but an upgrade—from measuring results to understanding processes, from rewarding output to rewarding cognitive growth.

### 4.5 Manager Self-Diagnosis

Before implementing cognitive management, managers must first answer three questions about themselves.

**Question I: Am I augmented or dependent?**

If you yourself can no longer function without AI writing your emails, reports, and making decisions, what right do you have to manage a team? A manager's cognitive pattern is the ceiling of the team's cognitive pattern. A dependent manager cannot effectively manage augmented employees—the cognitive gap between them becomes a power gap: the manager will use power to mask cognitive insecurity.

**Question II: Am I using "efficiency" to mask "capability degradation"?**

AI makes the team appear faster. But are the team's core capabilities strengthening or weakening? This question requires honest facing—efficiency improvement does not equal capability enhancement. If the cost of efficiency improvement is the degradation of the team's overall cognitive pattern, then this efficiency is unsustainable.

**Question III: Do I have the courage to make management decisions that "appear to reduce efficiency in the short term"?**

Constraining dependent employees will cause their output to decline in the short term. De-AI Days will cause the team to completely lose AI's efficiency加成 on a given day. These decisions appear "regressive" in the short term. Managers need the ability to explain the long-term value of these decisions to higher levels—otherwise, cognitive management will collapse under the first quarter's performance pressure.

These three questions have no standard answers. But if you dare not face them, the Cognitive Management Paradigm will remain a pretty framework that cannot be implemented.

## Closing: Paradigm Shift Is Not a Choice, It Is Inevitable

Thomas Kuhn proposed the classic model of paradigm shift in *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions*: when an old paradigm can no longer explain an increasing number of anomalies, the scientific community does not solve problems by patching the old paradigm—they undergo a paradigm shift, using a completely new conceptual framework to re-understand the world.

Management is undergoing such a paradigm shift.

Situational Leadership has explained countless management scenarios over the past fifty years. But the AI era has produced a series of anomalies the old paradigm cannot explain: output decoupling from capability, capability distribution shifting from uniform to bimodal, the same management actions producing opposite effects on different people.

You can try to patch the old paradigm—add "AI usage level" as a new dimension to the situational leadership model. But this only turns one problem into two. Because AI usage level and capability level are not orthogonal—they are intertwined, forming a two-dimensional space the old model cannot handle.

Paradigm shift is not a choice. When anomalies accumulate to a certain point, the old paradigm's explanatory power naturally collapses. The Cognitive Management Paradigm is not "another option"—it is the inevitable replacement after the old paradigm fails.

Kuhn wrote: "After a paradigm shift, scientists work in a different world." After management's paradigm shift, managers also work in a different world.

In this new world, management is no longer "matching management style to capability level." Management is "diagnosing cognitive patterns, then using opposite actions to push both cognitive patterns toward health." Management is no longer "finding the optimal solution and promoting it." Management is "finding optimal actions for each cognitive niche within cognitive heterogeneity, then letting them coexist in the same organization." Management is no longer "referee." Management is "translator"—building understanding between two cognitive patterns, finding balance in bimodal distribution, and guarding human judgment in AI's torrent.

Lin Wei eventually emerged from that dilemma. She did not find a "universal method" to manage both Zhang Wei and Li Zhe. She found two different management actions—delegation plus challenge for Zhang Wei, constraint plus awakening for Li Zhe. She built a culture of AI usage transparency in the team, designed mixed-grouping project structures, and introduced Cognitive Contribution Index performance evaluation.

Her team did not become more "unified." It became more "rich."

Looking back in three years, AI did not replace her team. AI reshaped her team—and the direction of reshaping depends on the management choices she makes today.

Paradigm shift is not a choice. It is inevitable. But within this inevitability, managers still have a choice—the choice to be a patcher of the old paradigm or a practitioner of the new one.
