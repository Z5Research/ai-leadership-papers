# The Evolution of Management Methodology: From Scientific Management to Cognitive Management — A Philosophical Evolution of Management Thought in the AI Era

---

## Opening

In 1911, Frederick Taylor stood on the shop floor of Bethlehem Steel, a stopwatch in his hand. He believed that as long as he could break down, measure, and optimize every movement of a worker, he could find the "one best way" to do the job. The stopwatch ticked away, recording the time for each shovel of iron ore, the angle of each bend, the distance of each cart push. What Taylor did not know was that this stopwatch was measuring more than time — it was anchoring a philosophy of management: efficiency comes from control, and the prerequisite of control is reducing the world to measurable variables.

One hundred and fifteen years later, the CEO of a technology company sat in a conference room, facing a strategic recommendation from an AI system. Accuracy: 97.3%. The recommendation was clear, data-rich, and logically rigorous. But he hesitated. Not because he distrusted the AI's judgment, but because he knew the AI would not be held accountable for the consequences of that recommendation. When the market shifted, when assumptions collapsed, when that 97.3% became 27.3%, the one sitting in that conference room bearing the consequences would be a human, not an algorithm.

From the stopwatch to the algorithm, management methodology has undergone four paradigm shifts. Each shift was not merely an upgrade of tools, but a re-answering of the question: "What is management?" Taylor answered the first question with his stopwatch: management is the optimization of movements. Weber answered the second question with bureaucracy: management is the maintenance of structure. Drucker answered the third question with his theory of knowledge workers: management is the unleashing of potential. And the AI era is forcing us to answer the fourth question.

There is no ready-made answer to this question. Because AI is not yet another management tool — it is a variable that changes the very nature of management itself. When AI can think, judge, and generate solutions, the core question of management studies shifts from "how to manage people" to "how to manage the cognitive relationship between humans and AI." This is not an iteration of management tools, but a reconstruction of management philosophy.

To understand the depth of this reconstruction, we need to return to the shop floor of 1911 and follow the intellectual trajectory of one hundred and fifteen years, seeing clearly how the philosophical foundations, core assumptions, and management actions of each paradigm shift have changed. Only by understanding where management methodology comes from can we understand where it is going.

## Part I: The First Paradigm Shift — Scientific Management (1911–1930s)

### Philosophical Foundations: Positivism and Mechanism

Scientific management was not Taylor's personal invention, but an inevitable product of the development of industrial capitalism in the late nineteenth century. When factory scale swelled from dozens to thousands of workers, and when production processes evolved from craft workshops to mechanized assembly lines, traditional empirical management — master-apprentice training, intuitive adjustment, authority-based maintenance — could no longer support the complexity of large-scale production. Taylor's contribution was to introduce the rising positivist methodology of the natural sciences into the factory shop floor, completing a cross-disciplinary transplant of methodology.

The core tenet of positivism is that only observable and measurable phenomena constitute the foundation of knowledge. Taylor applied this tenet to the field of management and arrived at a conclusion that was highly revolutionary at the time: management is not an art, but a science. The word "science" in "scientific management" was not rhetoric — it was a methodological declaration. Management problems could be solved through systematic observation, precise measurement, and experimental verification.

Running parallel to positivism was a mechanistic worldview. Nineteenth-century physics understood the universe as a precision machine, with every part having its definite position and function. Taylor applied the same logic to the factory: workers were parts in a machine, and the manager's task was to ensure that each part operated in the correct manner at the correct time. This analogy may seem cold today, but in the context of the time, it was a form of progress — because it meant that management no longer depended on the intuition of a brilliant manager, but could be built upon verifiable, replicable scientific methods.

### Core Assumptions and the Human Image

Scientific management was built on three core assumptions.

First, work is decomposable. Taylor believed that any complex work could be broken down into a series of basic movement units, each of which could be independently measured and optimized. In his pig-iron handling experiments at Bethlehem Steel, he decomposed the task of shoveling iron ore into five basic movements: bending, gripping the shovel, loading, turning, and throwing — then optimizing each one in turn. This reductionist view of work laid the methodological foundation for management methodology for the next century.

Second, there exists a "one best way." This is the most famous and most controversial claim of scientific management. Taylor argued that for any given work task, there exists one optimal method — not one among many good methods, but the single best one. The manager's task is to find this method through scientific analysis, then standardize it and propagate it to all workers.

Third, workers are "economic men" (Homo Economicus). The human nature assumption of scientific management can be traced back to the rational actor model of classical economics: humans are rational, self-interested, and their behavior is primarily driven by economic incentives. In Taylor's framework, the sole motivation of workers is to earn money, so the core mechanism of management is the differential piece-rate system — those who meet standards receive high wages, those who fall short receive low wages — to incentivize workers to adopt the "one best way."

These three assumptions together form the theoretical skeleton of scientific management. They may seem overly simplified today, but in the context of 1911, they represented a methodological clarity: rather than using vague concepts of "human nature" to explain management problems, it is better to use operational assumptions to construct verifiable management models.

### Management Actions: Decomposition, Measurement, Standardization, Incentive

The management actions of scientific management can be summarized in four keywords.

Decomposition. Taylor decomposed work processes into basic movement units and decomposed management functions into planning and execution. This decomposition had a profound impact: managers think, workers execute. The separation of mental and manual labor was not only an organizational principle of scientific management but also its epistemological premise — only by separating thinking from execution could the purity and scientific nature of thinking be ensured.

Measurement. The stopwatch was the iconic tool of scientific management, but the scope of measurement went far beyond time. Taylor measured the optimal load for each shovel (21 pounds), the optimal angle for each bend, the optimal distance for each cart push. The purpose of measurement was not surveillance, but optimization — finding optimal parameters through data and then solidifying them into standards.

Standardization. Once the "one best way" was found, the next step was to standardize it and enforce its adoption. Standardization was the critical link through which scientific management moved from theory to practice. It meant that management no longer depended on the experience and judgment of individual managers, but was built upon replicable, verifiable scientific standards.

Incentive. The differential piece-rate system was the incentive mechanism of scientific management. Its logic was simple and direct: if you work according to the standard method and achieve standard output, you receive high wages; if you do not follow the standard method or fail to meet output targets, you receive low wages. Incentive was not a favor — it was a necessary component of scientific management. Without incentive, there is no motivation; without motivation, there is no efficiency.

### Historical Contributions and Limitations

The contributions of scientific management are indisputable. It pulled management from the realm of experience into the realm of science, making management a discipline that could be studied, taught, and systematized. Driven by scientific management, U.S. industrial productivity achieved leapfrog growth between 1910 and 1930. Ford's assembly line, GM's Sloan-style divisions, and even the standardization ideas in modern quality management can all be traced back to Taylor's principles of scientific management.

But the limitations of scientific management are equally profound. It reduced humans to economic animals, ignoring human cognitive abilities, emotional needs, and social attributes. It reduced work to a collection of movements, ignoring the meaning and social dimensions of work. It reduced management to a control mechanism, ignoring the ethical and value dimensions of management.

These limitations could still be masked by the dividends of efficiency gains in the industrial age. But when the knowledge economy rose, when creative work became mainstream, and when employees were no longer satisfied with the logic of "work for money," the paradigm of scientific management began to reveal its internal tensions. The first paradigm shift completed its historical mission, but its limitations also sowed the seeds of the second paradigm shift.

## Part II: The Second Paradigm Shift — Bureaucratic Management (1922–1950s)

### Philosophical Foundations: Rationalism and Structural Functionalism

If scientific management answers the question of "how to do work well," bureaucratic management answers the question of "how to manage organizations well." These two questions may seem similar, but they are fundamentally different in nature: the former focuses on individual-level efficiency, while the latter focuses on organizational-level order.

Max Weber's theory of bureaucracy, proposed in *Economy and Society* (1922), was a systematic answer to the question of how large-scale organizations could maintain order and efficiency. Weber's starting point was sociology, not management — he was concerned with the legitimate basis of power in modern society. Among his three types of authority (traditional, charismatic, and legal-rational), legal-rational authority — authority based on rules and procedures — is the most stable and rational form of power in modern society. Bureaucracy is the organizational embodiment of legal-rational authority.

The philosophical foundation of bureaucracy is rationalism. Weber distinguished between instrumental rationality and value rationality: instrumental rationality focuses on the optimal matching of means and ends, while value rationality focuses on the legitimacy of the ends themselves. Bureaucracy is the ultimate expression of instrumental rationality — it does not concern itself with whether the organization's goals are legitimate (that is a matter of politics and ethics); it only concerns itself with how the organization can achieve its established goals in the most efficient and predictable manner.

Running parallel to rationalism was the sociological perspective of structural functionalism. Bureaucracy understands the organization as a structural system composed of roles, rules, and procedures. In this system, the value of an individual lies not in their personality or talent, but in their position and function within the structure. This structuralist view of the organization provided a theoretical foundation for the stable operation of large-scale organizations.

### Core Assumptions and the Human Image

Bureaucratic management is built on three core assumptions.

First, organizations need stable structures to ensure the continuity and predictability of decision-making. Weber observed that traditional authority (based on kinship and custom) and charismatic authority (based on personal charisma) both have inherent instability — the former is difficult to adapt to change, the latter is difficult to transmit. Only bureaucratic authority, based on rules and procedures, can maintain organizational continuity and stability amid personnel changes and environmental shifts.

Second, humans are "organizational persons" (Homo Organizational). Unlike the "economic man" assumption of scientific management, the human nature assumption of bureaucracy is more structural: human behavior is primarily determined by one's position in the organization, rather than by personal character or economic interests. The behavior pattern of an official in a bureaucracy is not determined by personal preferences, but by the role expectations prescribed by the position. This assumption elevates human behavior from the psychological level to the structural level.

Third, rules supersede judgment. The core principle of bureaucracy is that the operation of an organization depends on rules rather than personal judgment. Rules are universal, predictable, and accountable; personal judgment is particular, unpredictable, and difficult to hold accountable. In the logic of bureaucracy, the imperfection of rules is far preferable to the arbitrariness of judgment — because the former is at least knowable, debatable, and improvable.

### Management Actions: Hierarchy, Rules, Procedures, Delegation

The management actions of bureaucracy revolve around four core mechanisms.

Hierarchy. Bureaucracy designs organizations as pyramid-shaped hierarchical structures, with each level accountable to its superiors and wielding command authority over its subordinates. Hierarchy is not a symbol of power, but a mechanism for distributing responsibility — each level undertakes decision-making tasks of specific scope and complexity, thereby ensuring the efficiency and quality of organizational decision-making as a whole.

Rules. Rules are the lifeblood of bureaucracy. From recruitment standards to promotion criteria, from decision-making processes to communication channels, bureaucracy attempts to cover every aspect of organizational operation with rules. The density and precision of rules are seen as markers of organizational maturity.

Procedures. Procedures are the temporal dimension of rules — they prescribe the sequence, rhythm, and conditions of decision-making and action. The value of procedures lies not in whether they are optimal in themselves, but in their predictability and accountability. A procedurally correct but substantively wrong decision, in the logic of bureaucracy, is superior to a substantively correct but procedurally wrong decision — because the former upholds the authority of the institution, while the latter undermines its foundation.

Delegation. Power in bureaucracy is not personal but positional. Power derives from the position, not the individual — this is the core feature that distinguishes bureaucracy from traditional authority. The delegation mechanism ensures the replaceability and transmissibility of power — when a person leaves their position, power automatically transfers to the successor rather than departing with the individual.

### Historical Contributions and Limitations

The contribution of bureaucracy lies in its provision of a replicable management foundation for large-scale organizations. The giant organizations of the twentieth century — from General Motors' global supply chain to the U.S. federal government's hundreds of thousands of bureaucrats, from military chains of command to university academic governance — were all built upon the principles of bureaucracy. Bureaucracy enabled organizations to transcend the limits of individual capability and achieve stable, large-scale operation.

But the limitations of bureaucracy are equally profound. Its rigidity is an advantage in stable environments (ensuring predictability) but a disadvantage in changing environments (hindering adaptability). Its rule supremacism is a safeguard in routine situations (preventing arbitrariness) but an obstacle in exceptional situations (suppressing creativity). Its hierarchical structure is necessary in large-scale organizations (ensuring order) but a burden in small-scale innovation (increasing communication costs).

The deeper problem is that bureaucracy assumes the environment faced by organizations is relatively stable, tasks can be rule-based, and human behavior can be structured. But when the environment becomes uncertain, when tasks become complex, and when human creativity becomes the core of organizational competitiveness, the internal contradictions of bureaucracy are exposed.

The second paradigm shift established the management foundation for large-scale organizations, but it did not answer a deeper question: when organizations need innovation rather than stability, adaptation rather than control, unleashing rather than constraint, what should management do? This question would be answered in the third paradigm shift.

## Part III: The Third Paradigm Shift — Knowledge Worker Management (1954–2010s)

### Philosophical Foundations: Humanism and Epistemology

In *The Practice of Management* (1954), Peter Drucker put forward a proposition that was almost revolutionary at the time: knowledge workers cannot be managed by Taylor's stopwatch. This proposition may seem simple, but it contains one of the most profound paradigm shifts in the history of management studies.

The very definition of knowledge workers implies a double negation of both scientific management and bureaucracy. The output of knowledge workers cannot be decomposed into standardized movements — a software engineer's code, a marketing professional's creative proposal, a strategic consultant's business insight — the value of these outputs lies precisely in their inability to be standardized. The work process of knowledge workers cannot be externally observed and measured — thinking is internal, implicit, and non-linear. The motivation of knowledge workers cannot be achieved through differential piece-rate wages — the value of knowledge work lies not in "how much is done" but in "what is thought correctly."

Drucker's philosophical foundation was humanism. Unlike the mechanism of scientific management and the structuralism of bureaucracy, humanism views humans as subjects with internal drive, self-actualization needs, and creative potential. The task of management is not to control people, but to unleash them; not to constrain them, but to empower them. This humanistic philosophy of management pulled the focus of management studies back from "systems" to "people."

Running parallel to humanism was an epistemological turn. Drucker realized that the competitiveness of the modern economy no longer depends on the efficiency of manual labor, but on the quality of knowledge work. Knowledge, as a factor of production, has characteristics fundamentally different from manual labor: knowledge cannot be standardized, the production process of knowledge is invisible, and the value of knowledge is highly uncertain. This epistemological turn required management studies to establish an entirely new methodological framework.

### Core Assumptions and the Human Image

Knowledge worker management is built on three core assumptions.

First, the output of knowledge workers cannot be measured by standardization — it requires empowerment rather than control. This is Drucker's most core insight. Control is suitable for measurable, standardized work — you can measure a worker's movements with a stopwatch, constrain an official's behavior with rules. But empowerment is suitable for unmeasurable, non-standardizable work — you cannot measure the emergence of a creative inspiration with a stopwatch, constrain the formation of a strategic judgment with rules. Empowerment means providing resources, providing direction, providing feedback, and then letting knowledge workers make autonomous decisions within their areas of expertise.

Second, humans are "cognitive persons" (Homo Cognitivus). The human nature assumption of knowledge workers transcends both "economic man" and "organizational person": humans not only pursue economic interests, not only comply with structural roles, but also pursue meaning, growth, and self-actualization. Drucker famously wrote: "The productivity of knowledge workers will be the single biggest management challenge of the twenty-first century." The essence of this challenge is not how to improve the efficiency of knowledge workers, but how to stimulate their internal drive.

Third, the core question of management is stimulation rather than control. Scientific management asks "how to do it fastest," bureaucracy asks "how to manage it most stably," and knowledge worker management asks "how to stimulate it best." These three questions represent three different management philosophies: a philosophy of control, a philosophy of order, and a philosophy of empowerment.

### Management Actions: Goal Setting, Delegation, Feedback, Development

The management actions of knowledge worker management revolve around four core mechanisms.

Goal Setting. MBO (Management by Objectives) is the core management tool proposed by Drucker. Unlike the standardization of scientific management, the core of MBO is to translate organizational strategic goals into individual action directions through jointly set objectives between superiors and subordinates. Goal setting is not directive issuance, but consensus building — managers and knowledge workers reach agreement on "what constitutes valuable output," and then let knowledge workers autonomously decide "how to achieve this goal."

Delegation. Delegation is the core management action of knowledge worker management. Delegation is not laissez-faire, but bounded autonomy — managers set goals and boundaries, and knowledge workers make autonomous decisions within those boundaries. The prerequisite of delegation is trust: trust in the professional competence of knowledge workers, trust in their judgment, trust in their sense of responsibility. Delegation without trust is pseudo-delegation; delegation with trust is true empowerment.

Feedback. Feedback is the correction mechanism of knowledge worker management. Unlike the standardized correction of scientific management, feedback in knowledge worker management is bidirectional, dialogical, and development-oriented. Managers do not tell knowledge workers "what you did wrong," but discuss with them "how to do it better." The purpose of feedback is not punishment, but growth.

Development. Development is the long-term dimension of knowledge worker management. The value of knowledge workers lies in their knowledge capabilities, and knowledge capabilities require continuous updating and development. The manager's responsibility is not only to manage the current output of knowledge workers, but also to invest in their future capabilities. Training, job rotation, mentorship, cross-departmental projects — these are all development-oriented management actions.

### Representative Theories

The paradigm shift of knowledge worker management gave rise to a large number of representative theories, which enriched the theoretical framework of empowerment-based management from different perspectives.

McGregor's Theory X/Theory Y (1960) distinguished two management philosophies from the perspective of human nature assumptions. Theory X assumes that humans are lazy, need to be controlled, and lack internal drive — this is the human nature assumption of scientific management and bureaucracy. Theory Y assumes that humans are positive, self-driven, and have creative potential — this is the human nature assumption of knowledge worker management. The profundity of McGregor's insight lies in his observation that managers' behavior is largely a function of their assumptions about human nature — what kind of human nature you believe in determines what kind of management style you adopt. This is not a choice of management technique, but a choice of management philosophy.

Hersey and Blanchard's Situational Leadership Theory (1969) matched management styles with employee maturity. They proposed that there is no single "best" management style — management style should be adjusted according to the employee's competence and commitment levels. For employees with low competence and low commitment, managers need a "telling" style; for employees with high competence and high commitment, managers need a "delegating" style. The core insight of situational leadership is: management is not a fixed set of actions, but a dynamic matching process.

Mintzberg's Managerial Roles Theory (1973) discovered through empirical observation that a manager's work is not the planning, organizing, commanding, coordinating, and controlling described in textbooks, but a complex set of ten roles — figurehead, leader, liaison, monitor, disseminator, spokesperson, entrepreneur, disturbance handler, resource allocator, and negotiator. The profundity of Mintzberg's insight lies in his revelation that the essence of management is not the execution of fixed management functions, but the performance of multiple roles in complex situations. Management is a set of roles, not a checklist of actions.

Senge's Learning Organization Theory (1990) understood the organization as a cognitive system. Senge argued that organizational competitiveness lies not in the knowledge level of individuals, but in the learning capacity of the organization as a whole. The core mechanism of a learning organization is "systems thinking" — viewing the organization as an interconnected complex system, rather than a simple aggregation of departments. Senge's theory elevated knowledge worker management from the individual level to the organizational level: management is not only about stimulating individual creativity, but also about building organizational learning capacity.

### Historical Contributions and Limitations

The contribution of knowledge worker management was revolutionary. It re-introduced "humans" to the core of management studies, shifting the focus of management from "control" to "empowerment," from "efficiency" to "creativity." In the half century that followed, from flat organizations to agile teams, from OKRs to self-organization, from flexible work arrangements to remote work — every innovation in management studies has been an attempt to answer Drucker's question: how to unleash the potential of knowledge workers?

But knowledge worker management also has its limitations. Its core limitation lies in this: it assumes that team capability is evenly distributed. Situational leadership assumes that employee capability exists on a continuum — from low to high — and managers adjust their style according to where employees fall on that continuum. This assumption no longer holds in the AI era.

When AI tools are introduced into organizations, the capability distribution of teams shifts from a continuum to a bimodal distribution. Some employees achieve significant capability enhancement through AI, while others fall relatively behind due to their failure to use AI effectively. The difference between these two types of employees is not one of degree, but of mode. A single management approach harms both ends — applying "coaching" management to enhanced employees suppresses their creativity, while applying "delegating" management to dependent employees exacerbates their capability degradation.

Knowledge worker management did not anticipate this differentiation of cognitive modes. It assumed that variations in management actions only needed to match the level of capability, without realizing that management in the AI era needs to match the mode of cognition. This blind spot sowed the seeds of the fourth paradigm shift.

## Part IV: The Fourth Paradigm Shift — Cognitive Management (2023–)

### Philosophical Foundations: Cognitive Science, Complex Systems Theory, and Human-Machine Symbiosis

The arrival of the AI era is not yet another upgrade of management tools, but a fundamental shift in the object of management. The object of scientific management is movement, the object of bureaucracy is structure, the object of knowledge worker management is capability — and the object of cognitive management is cognitive modes themselves.

The philosophical foundation of this shift comes from the convergence of three fields.

Cognitive science reveals that the essence of human thinking is not information processing, but pattern recognition and meaning construction. The rise of AI — particularly the breakthroughs in large language models — has forced management studies to re-examine a fundamental question: when machines can process information, generate solutions, and provide recommendations, where does human cognitive uniqueness lie? Cognitive science's answer is: human uniqueness does not lie in the speed and precision of information processing, but in meaning understanding, value judgment, and creative breakthrough. The task of management shifts from managing information flows to managing meaning flows.

Complex systems theory provides a new framework for understanding organizations. Traditional management studies understand organizations as linear systems — inputs determine outputs, causes produce results, control brings efficiency. Complex systems theory understands organizations as non-linear systems — minor changes can trigger massive consequences, multi-factor interactions produce emergence, and control brings not only efficiency but also fragility. In the AI era, organizational complexity has not decreased but increased — because AI introduces new variables, new interaction patterns, and new sources of uncertainty. Management is no longer about designing systems, but about maintaining ecosystems.

Human-machine symbiosis theory is the distinctive philosophical foundation of cognitive management. It is neither the technological determinism of "AI replacing humans" nor the humanistic fantasy of "humans forever superior to AI," but a deeper understanding: AI is neither a substitute for humans nor a tool for humans, but an extension and reconstructive force of human cognition. When AI changes the way humans think, management must shift from "capability management" to "cognitive mode management" — because humans whose cognitive modes have been altered by AI are no longer knowledge workers in Drucker's sense.

### Core Assumptions and the Human Image

Cognitive management is built on three core assumptions.

First, AI changes the way humans think, and management must shift from "capability management" to "cognitive mode management." This is the most core proposition of cognitive management. AI does not simply enhance human capability — it changes human cognitive habits, cognitive strategies, and cognitive structures. A person who habitually makes decisions with AI assistance has a fundamentally different cognitive mode from one who does not rely on AI for decision-making. The manager's task is no longer to assess "what this person's capability level is," but to diagnose "what this person's cognitive mode is."

Second, humans are "cognitive persons" (Homo Cognitive-AI). This is the human nature assumption proposed by cognitive management. The cognitive person is not a purely human entity — their cognitive abilities, cognitive habits, and cognitive boundaries have all been redefined by AI. The cognitive person is also not AI — their meaning understanding, value judgment, and emotional connection remain uniquely human. The cognitive person is a new human form in the state of human-machine symbiosis: their capabilities are amplified by AI, their cognition is shaped by AI, their judgment is influenced by AI, but their responsibility remains human responsibility.

Third, cognitive heterogeneity within teams is inevitable, and management should not pursue uniformity but maintain diversity. In the AI era, teams will inevitably contain members with different cognitive modes — some view AI as a cognitive amplifier, some view AI as a cognitive substitute, and some oscillate between the two. This heterogeneity is not a management problem, but a management object. The task of management is not to eliminate heterogeneity, but to maintain the value of heterogeneity and manage the tensions of heterogeneity.

### Management Actions: Cognitive Diagnosis, Cognitive Matching, Cognitive Translation, Cognitive Ecology Maintenance

The management actions of cognitive management revolve around four core mechanisms.

Cognitive Diagnosis. Managers need to diagnose the cognitive mode of each team member — how they use AI, how they interact with AI, how they make decisions with AI assistance. This is not capability assessment, but cognitive mode identification. An employee with a high CLC (Cognitive Leverage Coefficient) and one with a low CLC may have the same professional capability, but their cognitive modes are fundamentally different — the former views AI as a cognitive partner, the latter views AI as a cognitive crutch.

Cognitive Matching. Managers need to match different management strategies to different cognitive modes. For enhanced employees (who view AI as a cognitive amplifier), managers need to "delegate + challenge" — granting more autonomy and assigning more challenging tasks. For dependent employees (who view AI as a cognitive substitute), managers need to "constrain + awaken" — setting bottom lines for AI use and maintaining basic cognitive capabilities. This is not situational leadership's "matching capability levels," but cognitive management's "matching cognitive modes."

Cognitive Translation. Managers are translators between different cognitive modes. Enhanced employees and dependent employees differ not only in capability, but also in cognitive language, decision-making logic, and communication styles. The manager's task is to build bridges of cognitive communication, preventing cognitive isolation — enabling members with different cognitive modes to understand each other's thinking logic, respect each other's cognitive choices, and find possibilities for collaboration amid differences.

Cognitive Ecology Maintenance. Managers are maintainers of the organizational cognitive ecology. The organization is not a power pyramid, but a cognitive ecosystem. In the ecosystem, different cognitive roles (leveragers, gatekeepers, translators) are interdependent, mutually constraining, and co-evolving. The goal of management is not control or empowerment, but maintaining ecological balance — ensuring that cognitive diversity is not swallowed by homogenization, ensuring that flow channels between cognitive roles remain open, and ensuring the resilience of the ecosystem.

### Four Dimensions of the New Methodology

#### Dimension 1: Cognitive Leverage

The core proposition of the cognitive leverage model can be expressed with a formula:

**Actual Output = Cognitive Habits × AI Leverage Coefficient (CLC)**

This formula may seem simple, but it contains three disruptive implications for management studies.

First, AI is not addition but multiplication. Traditional management tools — from Excel to ERP — are additive: they help employees complete existing work faster. AI is multiplicative: it changes the very nature of the work itself. An employee with good cognitive habits (skilled at asking questions, skilled at reflection, skilled at judgment) will see exponential growth in output when using AI; while an employee with poor cognitive habits (relying on intuition, refusing reflection, lacking judgment frameworks) will only accelerate the production of errors when using AI. What AI amplifies is not capability, but cognitive habits.

This asymmetry was validated in Microsoft's practice. After fully integrating Copilot into the Microsoft 365 ecosystem, Microsoft conducted an internal study covering tens of thousands of employees. The study found that the average output improvement of Copilot users was not evenly distributed: high-CLC employees saw output improvements of 3–5x, while low-CLC employees saw improvements of less than 50%, with some employees even experiencing judgment degradation due to over-reliance on AI. More critically, Microsoft found that the correlation between CLC scores and employee performance (r=0.68) was far higher than the correlation between AI tool usage frequency and performance (r=0.32). This means: what determines employee output in the AI era is not "how much AI is used," but "how AI is used."

Second, the Cognitive Leverage Coefficient (CLC) is a measurable management metric. CLC depends on four dimensions: judgment priority (the ability to maintain independent judgment under AI assistance), cognitive ownership (maintaining a sense of responsibility for AI outputs), metacognitive ability (the ability to reflect on one's own thinking process), and cognitive resilience (the ability to correct errors when AI makes mistakes). These four dimensions form a scale from 1 to 5, with the total score determining the employee's CLC level. CLC is not fixed — it can be improved through training, feedback, and cognitive habit coaching.

Third, Cognitive Debt is a new risk concept. Just as technical debt is the deferred cost of early technical decisions, cognitive debt is the hidden risk accumulated when employees over-rely on AI without developing their own cognitive capabilities. Cognitive debt does not manifest in short-term performance, but erupts collectively when organizations face AI system failures, AI output bias, or the need for creative breakthroughs. When deploying AI tools in financial analysis, Goldman Sachs used "Cognitive Leverage Return on Investment" (CLOI) as a core metric for investment decisions — AI tools with CLOI above 3x were prioritized for deployment, while tools with CLOI below 1.5x were deferred or redesigned. This investment decision framework centered on cognitive leverage enabled Goldman Sachs' AI investment efficiency to significantly exceed the industry average.

#### Dimension 2: Cognitive Heterogeneity

Traditional situational leadership theory assumes that team capability is a continuum — from low capability to high capability — and managers adjust their style according to employee capability levels. This assumption no longer holds in the AI era.

The uneven deployment of AI tools transforms team capability distribution from a continuum to a bimodal distribution. Some employees achieve significant capability enhancement through AI ("enhanced"), while others fall relatively behind due to their failure to use AI effectively ("dependent"). The difference between these two types of employees is not one of degree, but of mode.

The Cognitive Heterogeneity Index (CHI) is a metric for measuring the degree of AI capability differentiation within a team, defined as the ratio of the standard deviation to the mean of CLC scores in the team:

**CHI = σ(CLC) / μ(CLC)**

CHI > 0.5 indicates significant cognitive heterogeneity in the team, requiring the activation of dual-track management; 0.2 < CHI < 0.5 indicates the team is in a transition period, requiring attention to differentiation trends; CHI < 0.2 indicates relatively balanced AI capability in the team, allowing the use of traditional management methods.

JPMorgan Chase's practice provides a classic case of dual-track management. After deploying the COiN platform to process commercial loan document review, JPMorgan faced a typical dual-track management challenge: the AI track processed standardized documents at speeds far exceeding humans — reducing 360,000 hours/year of review work to seconds — but human lawyers remained irreplaceable in handling complex clause judgments and exceptional situations. JPMorgan's management innovation was the establishment of a "AI-first, human-gatekeeping" dual-track review mechanism. The AI track handles initial screening and classification, while the human track handles complex judgment. The two tracks are not in a simple serial relationship, but in a parallel verification relationship — AI and humans independently review the same document, then compare results, with discrepancies entering a third round of in-depth review. The effect of this dual-track mechanism was significant: error rates decreased by approximately 40%, review efficiency improved hundreds of times, and human lawyers' professional capabilities were strengthened rather than weakened with AI assistance.

The management value of cognitive heterogeneity lies in its provision of a quantified "dual-track management trigger point." When CHI exceeds 0.5, managers should immediately activate dual-track management — implementing "delegation + challenge" strategies for enhanced employees and "constraint + awakening" strategies for dependent employees.

#### Dimension 3: Cognitive Translation

Managers are translators between different cognitive modes. The theoretical source of this role can be traced to Boundary Spanning Theory and the Sociology of Translation.

Boundary Spanning Theory points out that information flow within organizations is not uniform — between members of different departments, different professional backgrounds, and different cognitive styles, there exist "cognitive boundaries." Individuals who cross these boundaries (boundary spanners) play key information bridging roles in organizations. In the AI era, cognitive boundaries exist not only between departments and professions, but more fundamentally between different cognitive modes.

The Sociology of Translation further points out that translation is not simple language conversion, but the reconstruction of meaning. When enhanced employees and dependent employees discuss the same problem, they differ not only in expression, but also in understanding, reasoning, and judgment criteria. The manager's translation work is not to make one side "understand" the other's words, but to help both sides understand each other's cognitive logic and find a basis for consensus amid differences.

Management actions for cognitive translation include: building bridges of cognitive communication (such as regular cognitive mode sharing sessions), preventing cognitive isolation (such as mixed-team mechanisms), and promoting cognitive mutual learning (such as having enhanced and dependent employees collaborate on projects). The goal of cognitive translation is not to eliminate differences, but to make differences a resource rather than an obstacle for the organization.

#### Dimension 4: Cognitive Ecology

Organizational design theory has evolved from "mechanical metaphors" to "organic metaphors" over the past century. Taylor and Weber's organizations were machines — parts were standardized, functions were replaceable, efficiency was predictable. Drucker and Mintzberg's organizations were organisms — they had adaptability, self-organizing capacity, and emergence. Organizations in the AI era need a new metaphor: the cognitive ecological forest.

The core proposition of the cognitive ecology model is: the organization is no longer a power pyramid, but a cognitive ecosystem. In the ecosystem, different cognitive roles (leveragers, gatekeepers, translators) are interdependent, mutually constraining, and co-evolving. The goal of management is not "control" or "empowerment," but "maintaining ecological balance" — ensuring that cognitive diversity is not swallowed by homogenization, ensuring that flow channels between cognitive roles remain open, and ensuring the resilience of the ecosystem.

DBS Bank's practice provides a typical case of cognitive ecology. In its "AI-First" organizational restructuring, DBS established a symbiosis mechanism for three cognitive roles: leveragers (employees skilled at using AI to amplify their cognitive capabilities, responsible for innovation and efficiency improvement), gatekeepers (employees who maintain a cautious attitude toward AI output, responsible for risk control and quality management), and translators (employees who can translate AI output into business language, responsible for cross-departmental communication and strategic alignment). DBS's key insight was: these three roles have no hierarchy of superiority — they are different functional positions within the ecosystem. Leveragers drive innovation, gatekeepers control risk, translators connect different cognitive worlds. A healthy cognitive ecosystem requires dynamic balance among the three roles — too many leveragers leads to失控 risk, too many gatekeepers leads to innovation stagnation, too many translators leads to weak execution.

The Cognitive Ecology Health Score (CEHS) is the core management metric of the cognitive ecology model, composed of three sub-metrics: Cognitive Diversity Index (CDI), Cognitive Role Balance (CRB), and Cognitive Flow Efficiency (CFE). CEHS = 0.4 × CDI + 0.3 × CRB + 0.3 × CFE. When any sub-metric falls below the baseline by 20%, management intervention is triggered.

The cognitive ecology model reveals a deep insight: the greatest organizational risk in the AI era is not "AI replacing humans," but "AI making everyone think in the same way." When everyone uses the same AI model, the same prompt framework, the same decision-making logic, the organization's cognitive diversity will rapidly degrade. And cognitive diversity is precisely the core capability of organizations in dealing with uncertainty — without cognitive diversity, there is no innovation; without cognitive diversity, there is no resilience.

| **Object of Management** | Movement | Structure | Capability | Cognitive Mode |

### Patterns of Change

The four paradigm shifts of management methodology are not a random sequence of events, but follow deep patterns of change.

**Pattern 1: The object of management moves from "outside" to "inside."** Scientific management focuses on external, visible movements — every movement of a worker can be measured by a stopwatch. Bureaucracy focuses on structure — organizational hierarchies, rules, and procedures are all visible and designable. Knowledge worker management focuses on capability — while capability cannot be directly observed, it can be indirectly measured through output. Cognitive management focuses on cognitive modes — this is the most internal, most implicit, and most difficult-to-measure object of management. The "internalization" trend of management objects reflects the deepening of management studies' understanding of "humans."

**Pattern 2: The manager's role evolves from "control" to "empowerment" to "translation."** The manager in scientific management is an overseer — supervising whether workers follow standard methods. The manager in bureaucracy is a bureaucrat — maintaining the authority of rules and the legitimacy of procedures. The manager in knowledge worker management is a coach — stimulating the internal drive of knowledge workers. The manager in cognitive management is a translator and ecology maintainer — building understanding between different cognitive modes, maintaining balance among diverse cognitive roles. The evolution of the manager's role reflects the shift of management power from "top-down control" to "bottom-up empowerment" to "horizontal connection."

**Pattern 3: The definition of efficiency shifts from "physical efficiency" to "cognitive efficiency."** The efficiency of scientific management is output per unit time — a physical, measurable metric. The efficiency of bureaucracy is the predictability of decisions — a structural, institutional metric. The efficiency of knowledge worker management is knowledge creativity — a cognitive, difficult-to-quantify metric. The efficiency of cognitive management is the cognitive leverage rate — a human-machine interactive, dynamically changing metric. The evolution of efficiency definitions reflects the shift of management value from "physical output" to "cognitive output."

**Pattern 4: Human nature assumptions move from "passive" to "active" to "redefined by AI."** The human nature assumption of scientific management is passive — workers work only for money and need to be incentivized to act. The human nature assumption of bureaucracy is structured — humans act within structures, and roles determine actions. The human nature assumption of knowledge worker management is active — humans have a drive for self-actualization and need to be stimulated rather than controlled. The human nature assumption of cognitive management is redefined by AI — human cognitive abilities, cognitive habits, and cognitive boundaries are all reshaped by AI. Humans are neither purely passive nor purely active, but cognitive persons in a state of human-machine symbiosis.

## Part VI: Philosophical Reflections on Management Methodology in the AI Era

### Question 1: The Ontology of Management Studies — What Is Management?

Ontology inquires into the essence of things. The ontological question of management studies is: what is the essence of management?

Taylor's answer was: management is the optimization of movements. The essence of management is to find optimal ways of working through scientific methods, then standardize and propagate them. The object of management is movement, the goal of management is efficiency, and the method of management is measurement and control.

Weber's answer was: management is the maintenance of structure. The essence of management is to maintain organizational stability and predictability through rules and procedures. The object of management is structure, the goal of management is order, and the method of management is institutional design.

Drucker's answer was: management is the unleashing of potential. The essence of management is to let knowledge workers发挥 their greatest creativity through empowerment and stimulation. The object of management is capability, the goal of management is innovation, and the method of management is goal setting and delegation.

The AI era's answer is: management is the maintenance of cognitive ecology. The essence of management is to maintain cognitive diversity, manage cognitive heterogeneity, and promote cognitive symbiosis in the state of human-machine symbiosis. The object of management is cognitive modes, the goal of management is cognitive leverage rate, and the method of management is cognitive diagnosis, cognitive matching, cognitive translation, and cognitive ecology maintenance.

These four answers are not mutually exclusive — they are cumulative. Management in the AI era still needs to optimize movements (the legacy of scientific management), still needs to maintain structure (the legacy of bureaucracy), still needs to unleash potential (the legacy of knowledge worker management). But management in the AI era has one additional dimension: the maintenance of cognitive ecology. This dimension is not a negation of the first three dimensions, but a transcendence of them — because in the AI era, the optimization of movements, the maintenance of structure, and the unleashing of potential must all be re-understood within the framework of cognitive ecology.

### Question 2: The Epistemology of Management Studies — How Do Managers Know?

Epistemology inquires into the conditions of possibility for knowledge. The epistemological question of management studies is: how do managers acquire the knowledge needed to make correct management decisions?

The epistemology of traditional management studies is built on two pillars: experience and data. Managers accumulate intuition through experience — this intuition is rapid pattern recognition in complex situations. Managers gain insights through data — these insights are quantitative understandings of organizational states. The combination of experience and data constitutes the foundation of managers "knowing."

The arrival of the AI era has disrupted this epistemological foundation. AI knows more — it can process massive amounts of data that managers cannot handle. AI knows faster — it can complete analysis while managers are still collecting information. AI knows more accurately — it has surpassed humans in pattern recognition and prediction accuracy.

So, what do managers know?

The answer is: managers know "why," while AI knows "what" and "how."

AI can answer "what" — it can provide data, identify patterns, generate solutions. AI can answer "how" — it can give optimal paths, predict outcomes, recommend actions. But AI cannot answer "why" — it cannot understand the dimension of meaning, cannot weigh value conflicts, cannot bear the consequences of decisions.

The epistemological uniqueness of managers lies not in information processing capability — in this dimension, AI has already surpassed humans. The epistemological uniqueness of managers lies in the ability to understand meaning — understanding the organization's mission, understanding employees' needs, understanding the value dimensions of decisions, understanding the ethical consequences of actions. These are irreplaceable by AI, not because AI is not smart enough, but because understanding meaning is essentially a human cognitive privilege.

### Question 3: The Ethics of Management Studies — What Should Management Pursue?

Ethics inquires into the legitimacy of values. The ethical question of management studies is: what values should management pursue?

The ethics of traditional management studies is built on three core values: efficiency, stability, and growth. Efficiency is the highest value of scientific management — the legitimacy of management lies in its ability to increase output. Stability is the highest value of bureaucracy — the legitimacy of management lies in its ability to maintain order. Growth is the highest value of knowledge worker management — the legitimacy of management lies in its ability to unleash creativity and drive innovation.

Management ethics in the AI era needs to add three new core values: cognitive diversity, human judgment, and cognitive autonomy.

Cognitive diversity is the core source of organizational resilience in the AI era. When AI provides "standard answers," the organization's competitiveness lies precisely in "non-standard answers." Cognitive diversity is not political correctness — it is an organizational survival strategy. Without cognitive diversity, organizations will lose their adaptive capacity in the face of uncertainty. Managers have an obligation to protect the organization's cognitive diversity — not out of moral sentiment, but out of rational consideration for organizational survival.

Human judgment is the ultimate bearer of management responsibility in the AI era. AI can give recommendations, but it cannot bear responsibility. When AI recommendations lead to catastrophic consequences, the ones bearing the consequences are humans, not algorithms. Therefore, managers have an obligation to maintain and enhance the organization's human judgment — not by restricting AI use, but by designing cognitive maintenance mechanisms (such as "AI-free days," cognitive training, cross-domain learning) to ensure that human judgment is not eroded by AI dependency.

Cognitive autonomy is the core guarantee of employee dignity in the AI era. When AI can replace large amounts of cognitive work, the greatest risk facing employees is not unemployment, but the loss of cognitive autonomy — when every decision of an employee is influenced by AI recommendations, when employees' thinking patterns are shaped by AI output, when employees' judgment is weakened by AI's precision, the status of employees as cognitive subjects is dissolved. Managers have an obligation to protect employees' cognitive autonomy, ensuring that AI is a cognitive partner rather than a cognitive master.

These three new ethical principles form the foundation of management ethics in the AI era. They are not a negation of efficiency, stability, and growth, but a supplement and correction to them — because in the AI era, efficiency without cognitive diversity is fragile, stability without human judgment is rigid, and growth without cognitive autonomy is alienated.

## Closing

One hundred and fifteen years. Four paradigm shifts.

From Taylor's stopwatch to AI's algorithms, from scientific management's "one best way" to cognitive management's "cognitive ecology maintenance," the path that management studies has walked is not a linear road from "backward" to "advanced," but a deep journey of return from "control" to "unleashing" to "symbiosis."

Each paradigm shift is not a negation of the previous one, but a re-answering of the question "what is management." Scientific management answered the question of movement, bureaucracy answered the question of structure, knowledge worker management answered the question of capability, and cognitive management is answering the question of cognitive modes. These four answers, layered together, constitute the complete answer of management studies to "what is management."

AI will not end management studies. But AI will end our old understanding of management studies.

Future managers do not need to understand AI's code. But they need to understand how AI changes human cognition. Because the next one hundred and fifteen years of management studies will not be about how to manage machines, but about how to relearn how to manage humans in the face of machines — understanding cognitive modes altered by AI, maintaining cognitive diversity threatened by AI, protecting human judgment challenged by AI.

The essence of management studies has not changed. It has never been the study of controlling people, but the study of unleashing human possibilities. In the AI era, this essence is not outdated — it is more important than ever. Because when AI can do more and more, what humans can do — understand meaning, make judgments, bear responsibility, create connections — becomes more precious, and more fragile.

The next chapter of management studies will be written by those who understand this.
